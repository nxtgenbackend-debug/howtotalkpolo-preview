Your first polo match: what to expect and what to bring

A polo match is an afternoon out with a fast game in the middle of it. Arrive early, bring a chair, and you’ll pick up the rest as you go.

How long is a polo match?

A chukker is about seven minutes of play. Most club matches play four of them, and the top level plays six. Add the breaks between chukkers, a longer halftime and the clock stopping for fouls, and a club match runs about an hour and a half to two hours from the first throw-in to the trophies. Many people come early and make an afternoon of it.

What happens at a polo match

  • Before the start: find a spot along the sideline and watch the ponies being readied on the pony line.
  • The throw-in: an umpire rolls the ball in between the two teams, lined up side by side, to start play. It happens again after every goal, and the teams switch ends.
  • Fresh ponies: between chukkers the players change horses, which is why one player brings several ponies to a match.
  • Halftime: the announcer invites everyone onto the field for the divot stomp, pressing the torn-up turf back into place.
  • The trophies: stay for the presentation after the last chukker. It’s short, and the players often stop to talk to fans afterwards.

Listen to the announcer throughout. Explaining the fouls and the players to newcomers is half the job, and it’s exactly what Don Lubner did from the booth at the Milwaukee Polo Club.

What to bring to a polo match

  • Chairs or a blanket. Many fields have little or no seating along the sideline.
  • Sun protection. A hat, sunglasses and sunscreen: there’s rarely much shade.
  • Water and a picnic, if the club allows food and drink. Many clubs welcome tailgating; check their rules on grills and glass.
  • Cash. Some clubs sell tickets or parking at the gate for cash only.
  • Binoculars. A full field is up to 300 yards long, and the play is often at the far end.
  • A layer for later, since late-afternoon matches cool off quickly.

For clothes and shoes, see what to wear to a polo match. For where to stand and how to behave around the ponies, read polo match etiquette.

Before you go

Check the club’s website or social media on the day. Rain cancels polo, because a wet field isn’t safe for the ponies. Still looking for a match? Here’s how to find polo near you. And if you want to follow the announcer’s every word, keep the polo terms and definitions open on your phone.
